Über das Stück · about the piece

This chorale is composed anew in the voice-leading manner of Johann Sebastian Bach: four real parts, a dignified cantus in the soprano, and a bass that carries the harmony like a continuo. It opens with an anacrusis and unfolds in four phrases of four measures, each sealed with a fermata, as a congregation would breathe between verses.

The tonal journey is the old Lutheran one: G minor is affirmed at the first cadence; the second phrase halts on the dominant D major; the third slips into the relative B♭ major by way of a deceptive resolution; and the last phrase returns home, closing on G major — the Picardy third, that sudden ray of major light at the end of a minor piece.

Devices of the old craft

A cadential six-four prepares the first close (m. 3). In measure 7 the bass climbs chromatically G–C–C♯–D beneath a descending soprano B♭–A–G–F♯, the C♯ sounding a secondary dominant (A⁷) that resolves, tone for tone, into D. Measures 9–10 spin a chain of prepared sevenths — C minor⁷ into F⁷ — whose expected resolution to B♭ is withheld: the F⁷ falls deceptively into G minor. Every dissonance is prepared and resolved; no parallel fifths or octaves occur between any pair of voices.

On the performance

Nothing here is sampled. Each note is built live from additive harmonics in the Web Audio API — a small principal chorus for the organ, formant-filtered voices with vibrato for the choir, plucked strings for the harpsichord — and sent through a generated church impulse response. Pull the stops, slow the tempo, and listen to the inner voices: the alto's E♮ against the bass's C♯ in measure 7 is the hinge of the whole piece.
